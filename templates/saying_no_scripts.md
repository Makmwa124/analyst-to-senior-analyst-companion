# Saying no, not yet, and yes if: scripts

*From Analyst to Senior Analyst, Chapters 6 and 8. Change the words until they sound like you; keep the structure.*

## The four-step structure

1. **Show you've understood.** Repeat back what they need and why.
2. **Say what's in the way.** Specific and factual.
3. **Offer one or two options.** Not yet, yes if, yes but smaller, or something better.
4. **Ask them to choose.**

## In conversation

**Not yet:** "I can't start this until [current work] is done on [date]. I could have it to you by [date]. Would that work?"

**Yes, if:** "I can do this by [date] if [other work] moves to [date]. That's [owner]'s, so I'd need to check with them. Or I can do it by [later date] without moving anything. Which do you prefer?"

**Yes, but smaller:** "The full version is about [effort]. If what you need is [smaller output] for [purpose], I can have that by [date], and we can decide afterwards whether the full version is worth it."

**No, but here's what I'd do instead:** "I don't think [request] will tell you [their real question], because [reason]. What I'd suggest instead is [alternative]. That's [effort] and should tell us [answer]."

## By email

> Hi [name],
>
> Thanks for this. I can see why [request] would help with [their goal].
>
> I'm committed to [current work] for [person] until [day], so I can't do [request] by [date]. Two options:
>
> - [Smaller version] by [date], and the full version by [later date].
> - Or the full version by [date], if [other person] is happy for [other work] to slip. I'd need to check that with them.
>
> Which would work better for you?

## To a senior person

Check with your manager first if you can. Then keep it short:

> "I can do this by [date], which would push [other work] to [date]. Or I can have it by [later date] with no knock-on. Which would you prefer?"

## Retiring a report

> "I've noticed [report] has been opened [n] times in the last [period]. I'd like to stop producing it from [date] and [replacement, if any], unless someone is relying on it. Could you let me know by [date] if that's a problem?"

## Holding the line on honesty

> "Happy to look at whether [X]. I'll report whatever the data shows. If it's positive, you'll have a much stronger case because it'll stand up to questions. If it's not, it's better to know now."

## When your manager volunteers you

> "I'm keen to do [new work], but my next [weeks] are committed to [current work]. I could start it on [date], or start now if [other work] moves. Or I could do a simpler first version by [date]. Which would you prefer?"

## The bad-news checklist (Chapter 8)

Before you deliver an inconvenient finding:

- [ ] **Am I sure?** Checked, weakest assumption tested, reviewed by someone else.
- [ ] **Who will be most affected?** Told them first, privately.
- [ ] **What's still true and useful?** Lead with that.
- [ ] **What's the decision?** Frame the finding around what to do next.
- [ ] **Have they had a chance to respond?** Anything they know that I don't?
- [ ] **Is the language neutral?** No "wrong", "failed" or "mistake" about other people's work.

## Owning your own mistake

> "I've found an error in [work]. [What happened, plainly]. The corrected figure is [x]. [Whether it changes the decision.] I've [what you've changed so it doesn't happen again]."
